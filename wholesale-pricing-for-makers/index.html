Wholesale Pricing for Makers: The 2×2 Rule, and When It Breaks

The standard wholesale pricing formula for makers is simple: wholesale price = full cost × 2, and retail price = wholesale × 2. It's a good starting point and it often produces a retail price your market won't pay. Here's how to use it, how to test it, and what to do when it breaks.

Selling wholesale to shops and stockists can turn a handmade business from one-at-a-time sales into batches of 24 or 48. It can also lock you into selling your best products at a price that barely covers your time. The difference is almost always made in how you set the wholesale price, before the first order.

The 2×2 rule

Wholesale price = full cost × 2

Suggested retail price = wholesale price × 2

"Full cost" here is the same figure you use for direct pricing: materials + other direct costs + your labour + overhead. If you haven't worked it out yet, how to price handmade products walks through it.

The logic:

  • Doubling full cost gives you a margin on every wholesale unit on top of your wage, with no platform fees eating into it (stockists usually pay by bank transfer).
  • Doubling again for retail reflects the common "keystone" arrangement, where a shop sells at about twice what it paid. Their markup pays for rent, staff, displays and unsold stock.

Applied to three products from our other guides (all made-up numbers):

ProductFull costWholesale (× 2)Suggested retail (× 2)Your own Etsy price at 35% margin
Bar of handmade soap$4.78$9.56$19.12$9.99
8 oz soy candle$10.40$20.80$41.60$19.99
Hand-thrown mug$21.26$42.52$85.04$39.99

Look at the last two columns. For every product, the 2×2 retail price is roughly double the price that already hits a healthy margin when you sell direct. That's the core problem with wholesale for handmade goods: there are two businesses taking a margin, and your labour is already a large share of cost.

The rule you can't break: retail price parity

Stockists expect you not to undercut them. If a shop sells your candle for $41.60 and you list it on Etsy for $19.99, the shop's customers will find your Etsy listing, and the shop won't reorder.

So you have three honest options:

  1. Raise your own retail price to the stockist's level. Only works if your direct buyers will pay it.
  2. Set retail at what the market pays, and work backwards to wholesale. Then check whether that wholesale price still covers your full cost.
  3. Don't wholesale that product. Offer wholesale only on items where the numbers work.

Option 2 is where most makers end up, so let's work it.

Working backwards from the retail price

Say candles like yours sell in local gift shops for about $24. With a keystone markup, the shop will want to pay about $12.

CandleFull costWholesaleMargin over full costAs % of wholesale
As made now (15 min each)$10.40$12.00$1.6013.3%
Batched (10 min each)$8.56$12.00$3.4428.7%

At 15 minutes a candle, a $12 wholesale price covers your $20-an-hour wage and leaves $1.60. That's not a loss, but it leaves little room for a mistake, a breakage or a slow-paying stockist. Batch production changes the picture: at 10 minutes a candle (pouring 24 at once, labelling in one session), the margin more than doubles. Wholesale orders are made for batching, so cost them at your batch time, not your one-off time. The candle pricing guide shows the batched calculation.

The soap tells the same story. If bars like yours retail around $9.99, the shop pays about $5.00. At a single-batch labour time the full cost is $4.78, leaving $0.22. Make four batches in one session and labour per bar falls enough to bring full cost to about $3.28, leaving $1.72 per bar at $5.00.

Your wholesale floor

Write down a floor for every product: the lowest wholesale price you'll accept. A sensible floor is full cost (at batch labour) plus a margin you choose, for example 25%:

Wholesale floor = full cost at batch time ÷ (1 − minimum margin)

Batched candle: $8.56 ÷ 0.75 = $11.41

If the market's wholesale price is below your floor, that product isn't a wholesale product for you, at least not yet. Better to know that before a stockist orders 48.

Terms that protect your margin

Price is only part of a wholesale offer. Your terms decide whether the price survives contact with real orders.

  • Minimum order quantity. Per product (e.g. 6 of each scent) and per order (e.g. $150 opening order). Minimums make batching possible.
  • Payment terms. Payment before dispatch for first orders is common for small makers. For repeat stockists, a short term like 14 days. Keep terms simple and clear. (Our invoices show payment terms but no interest or late-fee charges.)
  • Shipping. State who pays. If you ship for free above a threshold, include the postage in your cost.
  • Lead time. How many working days from order to dispatch, so you're not forced into rushed, expensive production.
  • Reorders. Lower or no minimum for repeat orders rewards loyal stockists without discounting the price.

What about consignment?

On consignment, the shop displays your work and pays you a split (often 60/40 or 70/30 in your favour) only when items sell. Your stock stays yours until then. Price it like retail: your share must cover full cost plus margin, and you carry the risk of unsold or damaged stock. Track consigned stock separately so it doesn't vanish from your inventory.

Building a line sheet

A line sheet is the one- or two-page wholesale catalogue you send to shops: product photo, name, SKU, wholesale price, suggested retail price, minimum quantity, and your terms. Keep it in sync with your pricing sheet, because a line sheet with last year's prices is a costly mistake.

  • Every product on it has a wholesale price at or above its floor
  • Suggested retail matches what you charge direct
  • Minimums, payment terms, shipping and lead time stated
  • SKUs match your inventory
  • Dated, so you know which version a shop has

Wholesale checklist

  • Full cost worked out at batch labour time
  • 2×2 prices calculated as a reference
  • Market retail price researched for comparable shops
  • Wholesale floor set for each product
  • Your own direct price equal to the suggested retail (no undercutting)
  • Minimums, payment terms, shipping and lead time decided
  • Line sheet produced from your current prices
  • Each wholesale order logged with channel "Wholesale" so its profit shows separately

Frequently asked questions

Is wholesale always 50% of retail?

It's a common convention, not a law. Some shops work on a slightly lower or higher markup. Ask what a shop expects, but always check the result against your floor.

Should my wholesale price include my labour?

Yes. Full cost includes your time at your hourly rate. Without it, wholesale can look profitable while really paying you nothing for the batch.

Can I offer a lower wholesale price for big orders?

You can, if the bigger order genuinely lowers your cost per unit (longer batches, bulk materials). Run the full cost again at that volume before you agree.

What if my 2×2 retail price is far above the market?

That's common for handmade goods. Work backwards from the market retail price, check the wholesale price against your floor, and focus on batching. If it still doesn't work, keep that product direct-only.
