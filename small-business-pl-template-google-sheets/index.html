Etsy Profit and Loss Template: A Monthly P&L for Makers (Google Sheets)

A profit and loss statement answers the question every maker eventually asks: after fees, postage, materials and everything else, what did the shop actually make this month? Here's the layout that works for small handmade businesses, a month worked through line by line, and the formulas to build your own in Google Sheets.

A profit and loss statement (P&L, or income statement) lists what came in and what went out over a period, and ends with one number: profit. For a small handmade shop, a monthly P&L is the single most useful report you can keep. It shows whether the business is earning, which months carry the year, and where money leaks.

Generic small-business templates often miss the costs that matter most to makers: platform fees, postage on orders, and materials. This guide uses a layout built around them.

The maker's P&L layout

SectionLineWhat goes in it

IncomeOrdersCount of orders (for averages, not money)
Cost of sellingPlatform & payment feesEtsy, Shopify, card reader and PayPal fees on each sale
Cost of sellingPostage on ordersLabels you bought to send orders

Two decisions shape the whole thing: how you treat materials, and whether you include a set-aside line. We'll take them in turn after the example.

Worked example: one month, line by line

Here's January for the fictional demo shop in our spreadsheet, a small candle and ceramics business. It uses the simple cash method.

LineJanuary
Gross sales$1,130.61
Orders34
Platform & payment fees$108.76
Postage on orders$213.79
Total cost of selling$322.55
Gross profit$808.06
Gross margin71.5%
Total operating expenses$533.90
Net profit$274.16
Net margin24.2%
Suggested tax set-aside (25%)$68.54
Profit after set-aside$205.62

Reading it from the top:

  • Fees were 9.6% of sales. That's in line with the Etsy defaults explained in Etsy fees explained. If that ratio jumps in a month, look for Offsite Ads fees or a change in channel mix.
  • Postage was 18.9% of sales. Compare it with what buyers paid for shipping. If you charged less than you paid, the gap is coming out of profit.
  • Operating expenses were $533.90. Under the cash method, this includes materials bought in January, which in a restocking month can be large.
  • Net profit was $274.16, before paying any tax. The 25% set-aside is a savings habit, not a tax calculation.

January is a quiet month for many gift-led shops. The value of a P&L shows up when you put twelve months side by side and see which ones carry the year.

Cash method or cost-of-goods method?

This is the most common source of confusion for makers keeping their own books.

Simple (cash) method. Materials are an expense when you buy them. Buy $400 of wax in March and March's profit drops by $400, even if the candles sell in May. Easy to keep, and it matches your bank balance. The downside is lumpy months: a big stock-up looks like a loss.

Product cost (COGS) method. Materials become a cost when the item sells. Each sale carries its own materials cost, taken from your pricing sheet, and purchases sit in inventory until then. Monthly profit is smoother and shows the true margin of what you sold, but you need product costs and a stock count.

Which should you use? For your own management reports, either works if you're consistent. For tax, the rules depend on your country and size, so ask a qualified accountant. Our cost of goods guide explains how to work out the per-item materials figure the COGS method needs.

The tax set-aside line

A P&L that ends at net profit tempts you to spend all of it. Adding a set-aside line, a fixed percentage of each month's net profit, turns it into a habit: move that amount into a separate account when you close the month. Many small sellers start somewhere around 20–30% and then ask an accountant for a better figure. The tax set-aside guide covers how to choose a percentage and what to do in loss months.

Build a simple P&L in Google Sheets

You can build a working monthly P&L from two log sheets. Here's the structure:

Sales sheet (one row per order): Date (A), Channel (B), Product (C), Gross sale including shipping charged (D), Platform fees (E), Postage paid (F), Status (G: Paid / Refunded).

Expenses sheet (one row per purchase): Date (A), Category (B), Amount (C), Supplier (D), Receipt saved? (E).

P&L sheet: months across row 1 as real dates (1 Jan, 1 Feb...), line items down column A. Then, for January in column B:

  • Gross sales: =SUMIFS(Sales!D:D, Sales!A:A, ">="&B1, Sales!A:A, "<"&EDATE(B1,1), Sales!G:G, "<>Refunded")
  • Orders: =COUNTIFS(Sales!A:A, ">="&B1, Sales!A:A, "<"&EDATE(B1,1), Sales!G:G, "<>Refunded")
  • Fees and postage: the same SUMIFS pattern on columns E and F
  • Each expense category: =SUMIFS(Expenses!C:C, Expenses!B:B, $A12, Expenses!A:A, ">="&B$1, Expenses!A:A, "<"&EDATE(B$1,1))
  • Gross profit, net profit, margins and the set-aside are simple arithmetic on those rows

Fill right for twelve months and add a total column. The EDATE pattern makes each column pick up exactly one calendar month.

Things a quick DIY version won't do on its own: work out fees from the channel automatically, cost materials per item for the COGS method, flag sales with a missing channel, or chart the year. That's where a ready-made workbook saves hours.

How to read your P&L each month

Five checks take about five minutes:

  • Net profit and net margin. Up or down on last month, and on the same month last year?
  • Fees as a % of sales. A sudden rise usually means ads or a channel change.
  • Postage paid vs postage charged. Are you quietly subsidising shipping?
  • Largest expense categories. Anything unexpected? Anything recurring you no longer use?
  • Set-aside moved? Transfer it now, while the month is fresh.

Once a quarter, look at the twelve-month view and ask which months carried the year. Plan stock, events and time off around them.

Common P&L mistakes for makers

  1. Recording payouts as sales. Your Etsy deposit is sales minus fees (and sometimes minus postage labels). Record gross sales and fees separately, or your fees disappear from view. The bookkeeping guide shows how to reconcile a deposit.
  2. Mixing personal and business spending. A separate account makes the expense log almost write itself.
  3. Forgetting refunds. Mark them so they drop out of sales.
  4. Switching methods mid-year. Pick cash or COGS and stick with it for the year.
  5. Counting your own wage as an expense. For a sole trader, what you pay yourself generally comes out of profit rather than being a business expense. Check with your accountant.

Frequently asked questions

Is a P&L the same as a cash flow statement?

No. A P&L shows profit for a period. Cash flow shows money in and out of the bank. Under the cash method they're close; under the COGS method, buying stock reduces cash but not profit until the stock sells.

How often should a small shop do a P&L?

Monthly. Log sales and expenses weekly, then close the month: check the totals, read the five numbers above and move the set-aside.

Can I use the same template for Etsy and Shopify and markets?

Yes. Add a channel column to your sales log. The P&L totals across channels, and a simple SUMIFS by channel shows where the money comes from.

Is this template accepted for tax returns?

A P&L is a summary of your records, and it can make filing easier. What your tax return requires depends on your country. Keep the underlying receipts and statements, and ask a qualified accountant.
