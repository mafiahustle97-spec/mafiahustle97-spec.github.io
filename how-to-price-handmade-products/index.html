How to Price Handmade Products (With a Free Calculator)

Pricing handmade products comes down to one formula: add up the full cost of one item, then divide by what's left after fees and your margin. Here it is step by step, with a ceramic mug worked through to the cent.

Most pricing advice for makers is a rule of thumb: double your materials, triple them, look at what similar shops charge. Rules of thumb are quick, and they're also why so many handmade products quietly earn nothing. They ignore your time, they ignore overheads, and they treat platform fees as if they came out of thin air.

This guide gives you a method instead. It takes about ten minutes per product the first time, and it gives you a number you can defend: to a customer, to a stockist, and to yourself on a slow Tuesday.

The handmade pricing formula

Every price you set has to cover five things: the materials in the item, any other per-item costs, your time, a share of your overheads, and the platform's fees. Whatever is left is profit. The formula puts those in the right order:

Full cost = materials + other direct costs + labour + overhead

Price = (full cost + fixed fees) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin)

Round up to .99. Write percentages as decimals: 9.5% = 0.095, 35% = 0.35.

The first line is what one item costs you to make. The second line turns that into a price where, after the platform has taken its percentage and its fixed fees, the amount left over is your target margin.

Notice the division. That's the part most makers get wrong, and we'll come back to why it matters.

Step 1: cost your materials properly

Materials cost is what you paid divided by what you got, multiplied by what one item uses. Do it per material, not as a guess for the whole item.

Our worked example is a hand-thrown ceramic mug sold on Etsy. All numbers are made up for illustration:

MaterialWhat you paidUsed per mugCost per mug
Stoneware clay$28 for a 25 lb (11,340 g) bag600 g$1.48
Glazebatch cost shared across mugs1 mug's share$0.90
Box, tissue and label$1.35 per set1 set$1.35
Materials total$3.73

The clay line is the one people skip: $28 ÷ 11,340 g = $0.00247 per gram, × 600 g = $1.48. It's tedious once and automatic after that, which is why a spreadsheet earns its keep here.

Include packaging. The box a product ships in is as much a cost of that product as the clay.

Step 2: add other direct costs

These are per-item costs that aren't materials: a share of kiln firing, a gift box upgrade, a care card printed per order. For the mug, we'll put $0.60 of electricity for two firings here.

Materials plus other direct costs gives a direct cost of $4.33.

Step 3: pay yourself for your time

Labour is minutes per item ÷ 60 × your hourly rate. Count all the minutes that belong to one item: making, finishing, photographing (shared across a batch), packing and labelling.

The mug takes 45 minutes once you add throwing, trimming, attaching the handle, glazing and a share of packing time. At $20 an hour, that's $15.00.

Which hourly rate should you use? Pick what you'd have to pay a competent person to do the work, or the rate you'd accept for skilled work locally. Don't use zero. A price that only works when your time is free is a hobby subsidy, and it stops working the day you get busy.

Step 4: add overhead

Overhead is everything you can't pin on one item: tools, the electricity for the studio lights, software, photography props, a share of your craft fair table. The simplest way to include it is a percentage of direct cost plus labour.

Start at 10%. Once you've kept books for a few months, you can work out your real figure: total yearly overheads ÷ the total direct and labour cost of everything you made that year. If overheads were $1,200 against $9,000 of direct and labour cost, your overhead is about 13%.

For the mug: ($4.33 + $15.00) × 10% = $1.93.

Step 5: add it up to a full cost

LayerMug
Materials$3.73
Other direct costs$0.60
Labour (45 min at $20/hr)$15.00

Full cost per mug$21.26

This is the number most shops never write down. If you only remember one thing from this guide: your materials are $3.73, but your mug costs $21.26.

Step 6: turn full cost into a price

Now apply the second line of the formula. On Etsy, using our US defaults as of November 2026, the percentage fees are 6.5% transaction + 3% payment processing = 9.5%, and the fixed fees are $0.25 per order + $0.20 listing = $0.45. (The Etsy fees guide explains each line.) We'll aim for a 30% target margin.

Price = ($21.26 + $0.45) ÷ (1 − 0.095 − 0.30) = $21.71 ÷ 0.605 = $35.88, which rounds up to $35.99.

Check it: at $35.99, Etsy's fees are $3.87, and $35.99 − $3.87 − $21.26 = $10.86 profit, a 30.2% margin. The formula did what it said.

Why you divide instead of adding percentages

The tempting shortcut is to take your cost and add 30% for margin and 9.5% for fees. Try it on a simpler item, the 8 oz candle from our candle pricing guide, with a full cost of $10.40 and the same $0.45 fixed fees:

  • Adding percentages: ($10.40 + $0.45) × 1.445 = $15.68. At $15.68, fees are $1.94 and profit is $3.34, a margin of 21.3%, not the 35% you wanted.
  • Dividing: ($10.40 + $0.45) ÷ (1 − 0.095 − 0.35) = $19.55, rounded to $19.99. Profit is $7.24, a margin of 36.2%.

Fees and margin are percentages of the selling price, not of your cost. Only division gets you there. Every percentage you add on top of cost leaks margin, and the leak grows as fees grow.

At $28 the mug isn't losing money, because your $15 wage is already inside the full cost. But the business keeps only $3.63 a mug for slow months, new equipment, breakages and growth. That's what "under target" means.

When the formula's price is higher than the market will pay

Sometimes the honest price is higher than buyers in your niche pay. Lowering your margin to zero isn't the answer. These are:

  1. Cut minutes, not your rate. Batching is the strongest lever most makers have. If the mug takes 30 minutes when you throw and glaze twelve at a time, full cost drops to $15.76 and the suggested price to $26.99. At $28 it now makes $9.13, a 32.6% margin.
  2. Buy materials in bigger units. A 50 lb bag usually costs less per gram than a 25 lb bag. Re-cost when you switch.
  3. Change the product, not the price. A larger size, a set of two or a gift-ready version often carries a better price-to-effort ratio.
  4. Sell where fees are lower. The same mug at a craft fair on a card reader pays 2.6% + $0.15 instead of 9.5% + $0.45. See craft fair profit per hour for how to judge a market.
  5. Retire it. Some products are lovely and can't earn their keep. Knowing that is useful.

What about checking competitors? Do it after you know your number, not instead of it. Another shop's price tells you what buyers might accept. It tells you nothing about that shop's costs, or whether they're making anything at all.

A pricing checklist for every product

  • Every material costed per unit (per gram, ml, piece or sheet), including packaging
  • Other per-item costs included (firing, inserts, gift boxes)
  • Labour counted in minutes, at a real hourly rate
  • Overhead included (10% to start, your real figure later)
  • Fees for the channel you'll sell on, including fixed per-order fees
  • Target margin chosen (the calculator starts at 35%)
  • Price rounded up, never down
  • Wholesale checked separately: see wholesale pricing for makers
  • Re-checked whenever a supplier price changes

Keeping prices right over time

A price is correct on the day you set it. Then wax goes up, postage changes, Etsy adjusts a fee, or you get faster at a product. Re-run your numbers whenever a cost moves, and at least once a year. The pricing mistakes guide covers the most common ways a good price drifts into a bad one.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good profit margin for handmade products?

There's no single right number. It depends on your product, channel and how much risk you carry. The free calculator starts at 35% after fees, on top of paying yourself an hourly rate. Treat that as a starting point, then adjust.

Should I include my labour if I'm doing this as a side business?

Yes. Leaving labour out makes a product look profitable when it's really being paid for with your evenings. If you later hire help, or simply get busy, the price has to cover that time anyway.

Is the "materials × 3" rule good enough?

Not for most handmade products. The mug's materials are $3.73, so "× 3" gives $11.19, about half its $21.26 full cost. At that price, after Etsy's fees, each mug would lose $11.58.

How often should I update my prices?

Whenever a material, fee or postage cost changes noticeably, and at least once a year. A spreadsheet that links your materials list to your prices makes this a two-minute job.
