10 Handmade Pricing Mistakes That Quietly Eat Your Profit

Most underpriced handmade products aren't the result of one big error. They come from small, reasonable-sounding shortcuts that each cost a little. Here are the ten we see most often, each with worked numbers and a fix.

Every example below uses the 8 oz soy candle from our candle pricing guide: $4.45 of materials, a full cost of $10.40 once you add 15 minutes at $20 an hour and 10% overhead, and a suggested Etsy price of $19.99 at a 35% target margin. At $19.99 it makes $7.24 a sale. Watch what each mistake does to that number.

All figures are examples, using our US Etsy fee defaults as of November 2026 (6.5% + 3% + $0.25 + $0.20).

1. Pricing from materials alone

The mistake: "Materials are $4.45, so I'll charge three times that: $13.35."

What it costs: at $13.35, Etsy's fees are $1.72 and the candle makes $1.23, a 9% margin. The rule ignored your time, overhead and fees, which together are bigger than the materials.

The fix: price from full cost with the formula: (full cost + fixed fees) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin). The complete method is here.

2. Paying yourself nothing

The mistake: leaving labour out "because I'd be making them anyway".

What it costs: without labour, the candle's cost looks like $4.90 (materials plus 10% overhead), and $12 looks like a healthy 46% margin. Count the 15 minutes and the same $12 leaves one cent on top of your $5.00 wage: at four candles an hour, that's 4 cents an hour more than your $20 rate. The price also collapses the moment you get busy or need help.

The fix: count minutes per item, honestly, and multiply by a real hourly rate. Then get faster by batching rather than by working for free.

3. Adding fees as a percentage of cost

The mistake: cost + 35% + 9.5% = price.

What it costs: ($10.40 + $0.45) × 1.445 = $15.68. At that price the margin is 21.3%, not 35%, because fees and margin are percentages of the selling price, not of your cost.

The fix: divide, don't multiply. ($10.40 + $0.45) ÷ (1 − 0.095 − 0.35) = $19.55 → $19.99, which gives 36.2%.

4. Forgetting fees on shipping

The mistake: pricing as if Etsy's fees only apply to the item.

What it costs: Etsy's transaction and processing fees also apply to the shipping the buyer pays. On a $5.95 shipping charge that's about $0.57 per order that wasn't in your numbers. See Etsy fees explained.

The fix: either set your shipping charge slightly above the label cost, or build the fee into the price. Check fees against your monthly statement.

5. "Free shipping" with no shipping in the price

The mistake: switching on free shipping to look competitive, without raising the price.

What it costs: at $24 with the buyer paying shipping, the candle makes $10.87. Absorb a $5.95 label at the same $24 price and it makes $4.92. Most of the profit went into the post.

The fix: add the average postage cost to full cost before running the formula. If the candle is $29.99 with free shipping, it makes $10.34, almost the same as before. Same buyer cost, same profit, better presentation.

6. Discounting without doing the math

The mistake: a 20% off sale "to get things moving".

What it costs: 20% off $19.99 is $15.99. Fees fall a little to $1.97, but profit falls from $7.24 to $3.62. A 20% discount halved the profit per sale. To earn the same total profit, you'd need to sell twice as many candles.

The fix: before any sale, work out the profit at the discounted price and the extra sales needed to break even. Prefer offers that protect margin: a gift-with-purchase that costs you $1, a bundle priced from its full cost, or free shipping above a threshold that's built into your prices.

7. Copying a competitor's price

The mistake: "Similar candles sell for $14, so mine will too."

What it costs: you've adopted someone else's costs, speed, fees and goals without knowing any of them. They may buy wax by the pallet, pour 200 a day, or simply not be making money. At $14, Etsy's fees are $1.78 and your candle makes $1.82.

The fix: know your own number first. Then use competitors to judge whether your number is realistic, and if it isn't, change the product, your costs or your channel, not just the price. Some products don't work at the market's price for your setup, and knowing that is valuable.

8. Never updating prices when costs rise

The mistake: pricing once, at launch, and leaving it.

What it costs: if your wax goes from $32 to $38 a bag, materials rise from $4.45 to $4.72 and profit at $19.99 drops from $7.24 to $6.95. Small on one candle. Repeated across wax, jars, fragrance, postage and a fee change, a "fine" price drifts into an "under target" one without anyone noticing.

The fix: keep supplier prices in one place linked to every product, and review prices whenever a cost moves or at least once a year. The cost of goods guide shows how to set this up.

9. Ignoring the occasional extra fee

The mistake: pricing as if every sale had the standard fees.

What it costs: when an Etsy sale is attributed to Offsite Ads, an extra 12% or 15% applies. At 15% on a $19.99 candle that's $3.00, and profit on that order falls from $7.24 to $4.24. If a large share of your sales come through ads, your real average margin is lower than your pricing sheet says.

The fix: look at last year's statements to see how much ads cost as a share of sales, and make sure your target margin can absorb it. Track ad fees as their own line in your books.

10. One price, every channel, no wholesale check

The mistake: setting a single retail price and then saying yes to the first stockist who asks for "the usual 50% off".

What it costs: at a $19.99 retail price, half is $10.00, which is below the candle's $10.40 full cost. Every wholesale candle loses money once your time is counted.

The fix: decide your wholesale floor for each product before anyone asks, using full cost at batch speed. Keep your own retail price in line with what stockists will charge. The wholesale pricing guide works through it.

Quick audit: check your prices in 20 minutes

  • Pick your five best-selling products
  • For each, write materials, minutes, overhead and the channel's fees
  • Work out full cost and the suggested price at your target margin
  • Compare with your current price: losing money, under target or on target?
  • Check shipping: is the postage you pay covered by what you charge or by the price?
  • Check your last sale or discount: what did profit per sale actually drop to?
  • Write a wholesale floor for any product you might wholesale
  • Put a date in your calendar to repeat this in six months

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common handmade pricing mistake?

Leaving out labour, closely followed by pricing from a multiple of materials. The two usually go together, because a materials multiple hides the fact that your time isn't counted.

Is it ever OK to sell below full cost?

As a deliberate, counted choice, yes: clearing old stock, or a small sample size that introduces buyers to your range. The mistake is doing it without knowing.

How do I raise prices without losing customers?

Raise them when you have a reason you can stand behind (new materials costs, better packaging), move in modest steps, and keep your quality visible. Many makers find a fair price attracts the customers who value the work.

Should I use charm pricing like $19.99?

It's a common convention, and our calculator rounds up to .99 by default. What matters more is rounding up, so the rounding never comes out of your margin.
