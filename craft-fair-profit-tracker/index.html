Craft Fair Profit Tracker: Is Your Market Worth It?

A busy craft fair can feel like a great day and still pay less per hour than staying home to fill online orders. The fix is one number per event, profit per hour, compared with the hourly rate you price your products at. Here's how to work it out.

"How did the fair go?" is usually answered with a takings figure: "I did $640!" Takings are the least useful number from a market day. They don't subtract the booth fee, the card reader's cut, the stock you sold at cost, the fuel and parking, or the twelve hours the day really took once you count packing the car the night before.

A craft fair profit tracker answers a better question: for every hour this event took, how much did the business keep? Compare that with the hourly rate you already use to price your products, and you have a clear rebook-or-rethink decision.

The profit-per-hour formula

Net profit = sales − card fees − materials cost of items sold − event costs

Profit per hour = net profit ÷ total hours

Rebook if profit per hour ≥ your hourly rate. Otherwise, rethink.

Each part needs a little care.

Sales

Total takings: cash, card and anything paid by bank transfer on the day. If you take a deposit for a custom order at the fair, count it when the order is completed, the same way you would online.

Card fees

In-person card readers typically charge a percentage plus a small fixed fee per transaction. Our default preset is 2.6% + $0.15 per tap, but check your own provider. Cash sales have no fee, though they do need counting and banking.

Materials cost of items sold

What the stock you sold cost you in materials and packaging, from your pricing sheet. Use materials, not full cost: your labour is what we're measuring with profit per hour, so including it would count it twice. Unsold stock comes home and isn't a cost of this event.

Event costs

Everything you spent because of this event:

  • Booth or pitch fee, and any application or jury fee
  • Fuel, parking, tolls or public transport
  • Table cover, signage or display items bought for this fair (or a fair share, if you'll reuse them)
  • Bags, wrapping and receipt paper used on the day
  • Food you wouldn't otherwise have bought, if your accountant agrees it counts

Total hours

This is the one people undercount. Include:

  • Preparing stock specifically for this event (extra tagging, bagging, building gift sets)
  • Packing and loading the car
  • Travel both ways
  • Set-up and pack-down
  • The selling hours themselves
  • Unloading and restocking at home

A "seven-hour market" is often a twelve-hour day.

The art walk had respectable takings, but a $150 pitch fee, $24 of travel and $12 of bags, plus eleven hours, left $8.14 an hour. The business would have done better at home making stock for Etsy.

The two-day fair took the most money and the most hours. Its profit per hour is almost identical to the Saturday market. Bigger isn't automatically better; it's just bigger.

Why compare with your hourly rate?

Your hourly rate is already in every product price (see how to price handmade products). If a market pays you less per hour than that rate, every hour there is an hour you'd have earned more by making and selling online at your normal prices, assuming you have the demand.

It also turns a feeling into a rule. "It was a lovely day but quiet" and "I think it went OK?" become "$8.14 an hour, below my $20 rate: rethink."

What "rethink" can mean

A Rethink verdict doesn't always mean "never again". It means change something, then measure again:

  1. Cut hours. Could you share a stall and alternate days? Pre-pack the car display so set-up takes 30 minutes, not 90?
  2. Raise the average sale. Bundles and gift sets at a market often lift the takings per customer. Price them properly first; our pricing mistakes guide explains why discounts on sets can backfire.
  3. Negotiate or swap the pitch. A cheaper pitch at the same event, or a smaller event closer to home.
  4. Reduce card costs. Compare card reader rates. Don't push buyers to cash if it costs you sales.
  5. Count the non-cash value, honestly. Did you take wholesale enquiries or custom orders? Track them separately and see whether they actually turn into sales. Don't let "exposure" become the reason to rebook a fair that never pays.

First-year events: give them two tries

One wet Saturday can sink any market. If a new event shows Rethink, look at why before you drop it: weather, a clash with another event, a poor pitch position. A reasonable rule is to rebook a new event once, then let the numbers decide.

How to track a market in your books

To work out profit per hour after the event, your records need to tie sales and costs to the event. The simplest method is a tag.

  • Give each event a short name, e.g. "Spring Makers Market"
  • Log every sale from the day with that name as a tag, channel "Craft fair (card reader)" or "Craft fair (cash)"
  • Log the booth fee, travel and event-only purchases as expenses with the same tag
  • Note total hours, including prep and travel, the evening after
  • Count the stock you brought home, so your inventory stays right

Pricing at markets versus online

Should prices be lower at a fair because there are no Etsy fees? Usually, no. Keep prices consistent across channels so online customers don't feel short-changed and stockists aren't undercut. The fee saving at a market is real, but it's partly paying for your booth fee, travel and time. The per-item formula on the free calculator will show a lower suggested price for the card-reader channel; treat that as information about margin, not an instruction to discount.

Preparing for the next season

At the end of the year, sort your events by profit per hour. Most makers find a clear top tier worth booking early, a middle tier worth one more try with a change, and one or two to let go. That list is one of the most valuable planning documents a market seller can have, and it only exists if you track each event.

Frequently asked questions

Is a craft fair worth it if I only break even?

If "break even" means net profit after fees, stock and event costs is about zero, then you worked the whole day unpaid. Unless you can point to orders or stockists that came from it, it's a Rethink.

Should I include my hourly wage as a cost?

No. Net profit here is before paying yourself, and profit per hour is what you effectively earned. Adding your wage as a cost and then dividing by hours would count it twice.

What about stock I made specifically for the fair but didn't sell?

It isn't a cost of this fair. It goes back into inventory at its materials cost and becomes a cost when it sells. Count the extra prep time, though.

Can I use this for pop-ups and wholesale trade shows?

Yes. Any event with a fee, travel and a block of your time works the same way. For trade shows, track orders taken as well, because the sales arrive later.
