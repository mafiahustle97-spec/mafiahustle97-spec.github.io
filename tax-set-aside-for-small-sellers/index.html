How Much to Set Aside for Tax as an Etsy or Handmade Seller

The easiest way to avoid a painful tax bill is to set money aside every month, as a percentage of profit rather than sales, in an account you don't touch. Here's how to choose a starting percentage, a worked example, and a tracker that shows if you've fallen behind.

This is not tax advice

This guide describes a savings habit, not a tax calculation. How much tax you owe depends on your country, your total income, your expenses, allowances and your personal situation. Please check with a qualified accountant or your tax authority. We mention a few common rules as examples only.

Many small sellers get their first tax bill and discover the money has already been spent: on stock, on equipment, or just on life. Nothing went wrong in the business. The cash simply wasn't put somewhere safe as it came in.

A tax set-aside fixes that. Each month, you take a percentage of the month's profit and move it into a separate account. When the bill arrives, the money is waiting.

Why profit, not sales?

Income tax for a self-employed seller is generally charged on profit: sales minus allowable business expenses. Setting aside a percentage of sales overshoots in good-margin months and can't adapt to a month when you bought a lot of stock.

Compare two months for the same shop:

Month AMonth B
Gross sales$2,000$2,000
Fees, postage, materials and expenses$1,200$1,700
Net profit$800$300
25% of profit$200$75
10% of sales (for comparison)$200$200

In Month B, a sales-based rule would set aside $200 against a profit of only $300. A profit-based rule tracks what the business actually earned.

(If you're registered for VAT or GST, or collect sales tax yourself, that's a different matter: those amounts are usually based on sales and belong to the tax authority, not to you. Keep them separate from this habit, and ask an accountant.)

Choosing a starting percentage

There isn't a universal right number, and we won't pretend there is. What you can do:

  1. Start with a reasonable round number. Many small sellers begin somewhere in the 20–30% range of net profit while they find out their real position. It's a starting point, not a recommendation for your situation.
  2. Ask for a better number. Once you have a few months of P&L figures, an accountant can estimate your likely tax and tell you what percentage to use.
  3. Adjust after your first return. Compare what you set aside with what you actually owed, and change the percentage for next year.
  4. Remember the extras. In some countries, self-employed people pay social contributions on top of income tax (in the US, self-employment tax; in the UK, National Insurance for the self-employed). Others have payment deadlines during the year. Your percentage should cover the total.

Erring slightly high is usually more comfortable than slightly low: leftover set-aside is a pleasant surprise, a shortfall isn't.

Worked example: a month in the demo shop

The fictional demo shop in our spreadsheet uses a 25% set-aside. Here's March:

At month end: move $210.52 to the tax account. Done. The P&L guide shows how to get the net profit figure each month.

What about loss months?

If a month's net profit is zero or negative, the suggested set-aside is zero. Don't take money out of the tax account to cover it; profitable months later in the year will usually make up the difference, and your tax is generally worked out on the whole year. If the year as a whole ends lower than expected, your accountant can tell you whether you've set aside more than you need.

Track planned versus moved

A set-aside only works if the money actually moves. Keep a small tracker:

MonthNet profitSuggested set-asideMovedRunning gap
Jan$274.16$68.54$100.00−$31.46
Feb$557.06$139.27$100.00$7.81
Mar$842.06$210.52$200.00$18.33

The running gap is total suggested minus total moved. A positive gap means you're behind; a negative gap means you're ahead. Many people move a round number each month and top up when the gap grows. Check it each quarter.

Where to keep it

  • A separate account that you don't use for spending. Some people keep it at a different bank entirely so it's out of sight.
  • Not in stock or equipment. Money tied up in materials can't pay a tax bill.
  • Name it. "Tax, do not touch" in your banking app sounds silly and works.

If you prefer to avoid interest for religious or personal reasons, a plain current account or a separate pot without interest does the job just as well. The point is separation, not growth.

Key dates to put in your calendar

Deadlines vary by country, so check yours. As examples only:

  • US: many self-employed people make quarterly estimated tax payments, and the annual return is usually due in April.
  • UK: the Self Assessment online filing and payment deadline is 31 January after the end of the tax year (which ends on 5 April), and some people also make payments on account.
  • Canada and Australia have their own annual deadlines and, for some, instalments.

Whatever your dates, your quarterly set-aside check should come a few weeks before each payment.

A note on zakat

If you pay zakat, it's calculated differently from tax: broadly on qualifying business wealth (such as cash, money owed to you and trade stock, less short-term debts) once a year, rather than on profit. Keep it separate from your tax set-aside. Four set-aside mistakes to avoid

  1. Setting aside from the payout. Your Etsy deposit is sales minus fees and labels, but your materials, packaging, ads and other expenses haven't come off yet. A percentage of the payout overshoots in a restocking month and can undershoot in a month when Etsy pays out late. Use net profit from your P&L.
  2. Leaving out cash and market sales. If craft fair takings or Instagram orders aren't in your books, they aren't in your profit or your set-aside, but they may still be taxable. Log every channel; the bookkeeping guide shows a 15-minute weekly routine.
  3. Borrowing from the tax account. A slow month, a big wax order, a new kiln: it's tempting to dip in and "put it back later". Later is usually the month before the bill. If you genuinely need the money, write the amount down as a gap in your tracker and close it first, before you pay yourself.
  4. Never revisiting the percentage. Your first percentage is a guess. After your first return, compare what you set aside with what you actually owed, and change the number. If your profit grows a lot, check again sooner, because in many tax systems the rate on extra profit is higher than on the first slice.

Monthly set-aside checklist

  • Month's sales and expenses logged (weekly routine)
  • Net profit read from the P&L
  • Set-aside percentage applied (zero in a loss month)
  • Amount moved to the separate account
  • Moved amount recorded in the tracker
  • Running gap checked; top up if it's growing
  • Quarterly: compare with any upcoming tax payment dates

Frequently asked questions

What percentage should I set aside for taxes on Etsy sales?

It depends on your country and income, so ask an accountant for your number. Many small sellers start in the 20–30% range of net profit, then adjust once they know their real position.

Do I set aside tax on my Etsy payout?

No, on your profit. A payout is sales minus fees and labels, but you still have materials and other expenses to subtract. Use the net profit from your P&L.

Is the set-aside the tax I owe?

No. It's a savings habit that gives you money ready for whatever you owe. The actual amount comes from your tax return.

I'm a hobby seller. Do I need this?

Whether your sales count as a business for tax depends on where you live and how you sell. If you're unsure, ask, and keep records and a set-aside in the meantime. It costs nothing to have the money ready.
