Cost of Goods for Handmade Products: How to Work Out COGS

Cost of goods sold (COGS) is what the materials in the items you sold cost you. For makers it's the foundation of both pricing and bookkeeping, and it's easy to get wrong in small ways that add up. Here's how to work it out per item, with a soap batch costed to the cent, and how it fits into your books.

Ask a maker what a product costs and you'll usually get a rough figure for the main material. Ask what last month's sales cost in materials and most can't say. Both questions are about cost of goods, and both are answerable with a little structure.

This guide covers two related ideas:

  • Cost per item: what the materials and packaging in one finished item cost. You need it to price.
  • Cost of goods sold (COGS) for a period: the total materials cost of everything you sold that month or year. You need it for an accurate profit figure if you use the cost-of-goods method.

COGS versus full cost

These get mixed up, and the difference matters.

Cost of goods (materials)Full cost (for pricing)
Materials and ingredients✓✓
Packaging that ships with the product✓✓
Per-item extras (inserts, firing share)usually✓
Your own timegenerally not, for a sole trader✓
Overhead shareno✓

Full cost is a pricing tool: it makes sure your price pays you for your time and covers overheads. COGS is a bookkeeping figure. For a sole trader, your own labour generally isn't a business expense; it's what profit pays you. Employees' wages and some production costs can be treated differently, so check with an accountant how your country treats them.

Use full cost to set prices (how to price handmade products). Use materials cost per item for COGS.

Step 1: turn every purchase into a unit cost

Every material needs a cost per unit you actually measure in: per gram, per ml, per piece, per sheet, per metre.

Unit cost = what you paid (including delivery) ÷ quantity received

Include delivery and any import charges in "what you paid". A $24 bag of oil with $6 delivery is a $30 bag.

Watch the units. If you buy by the pound and measure in grams, convert once (1 lb = 453.6 g) and record the gram price. If a liquid is sold by volume but you weigh it into a recipe, either measure it by volume or weigh a known volume once to find its density.

Step 2: write a recipe (bill of materials)

A recipe lists exactly what goes into one batch or one item. Here's a made-up batch of cold-process soap:

Coconut oil$19 for 1,400 g250 g$3.39
Shea butter$15 for 1,000 g100 g$1.50

Fragrance oil$18 for 236 ml30 ml$2.29
Colourantper batch1 batch$0.40
Batch total$14.30

Water isn't costed here because its cost is negligible. If something you use has a real cost, include it, however small.

Step 3: divide by what you can actually sell

The batch is cut into 12 bars. One is lost to trimming the ends and a test bar. So the batch yields 11 sellable bars, not 12.

  • Divided by 12: $14.30 ÷ 12 = $1.19 per bar
  • Divided by 11: $14.30 ÷ 11 = $1.30 per bar

That 11-cent difference is waste, and it's real money. Across every batch you make in a year it adds up. Always divide by sellable yield. If one in twenty candles is a second, or one in ten mugs cracks in the kiln, the survivors carry the cost of the losses.

Step 4: add packaging

Packaging that leaves with the product belongs in its cost:

ItemPer bar
Soap (batch ÷ 11)$1.30

Materials per bar (COGS per item)$1.62

Shipping boxes and void fill used per order (rather than per item) are usually easier to record as postage and shipping supplies expenses, or to include in the shipping you charge.

From cost per item to a price

For pricing, add your time and overhead. If the batch takes 90 minutes including mixing, pouring, cutting, wrapping and cleaning, that's 90 ÷ 11 ≈ 8.2 minutes per bar:

Materials and packaging$1.62
Labour (8.2 min at $20/hr)$2.73
Overhead (10%)$0.43
Full cost$4.78

COGS in your books: two ways to get the period total

If you use the cost-of-goods method in your profit and loss (see the P&L guide), you need total COGS for each month. There are two ways to get it.

Method 1: per-item costs × units sold

Record each product's materials cost per item (as above), then for each sale multiply by quantity. Five bars sold = 5 × $1.62 = $8.10 of COGS. A spreadsheet can do this automatically if each sale names the product. This gives you profit by product, which is the most useful view for pricing decisions.

Method 2: stock counts

Count and value your materials and finished stock at the start and end of the period:

COGS = opening stock + purchases − closing stock

Example for a quarter: opening stock $380 + materials bought $920 − closing stock $450 = $850 of COGS.

This method catches things per-item costing misses: waste you didn't record, failed batches, samples, theft or a spill. Many businesses use per-item costs month to month and a stock count at year end, then investigate any big difference between the two.

Keeping costs current

Unit costs go stale. A supplier raises prices, you switch to a bigger bag, delivery charges change. If wax goes from $32 to $38 a bag, the 8 oz candle in our candle guide goes from $4.45 to $4.72 in materials, and its profit at $19.99 drops from $7.24 to $6.95. Small per item; across a year of candles, worth knowing.

The fix is structural: keep each supplier price in one place and have every recipe point to it. Change the wax price once and every product that uses wax updates. COGS checklist

  • Every material has a unit cost including delivery
  • Units match how you measure (grams, ml, pieces)
  • A recipe for every product or batch
  • Batch cost divided by sellable yield, not the theoretical number
  • Packaging that ships with the item included
  • Your labour in full cost for pricing, but not in COGS for your books (check locally)
  • Unit costs updated when supplier prices change
  • A stock count at least once a year

Frequently asked questions

Is my time part of cost of goods sold?

For pricing, always include it, in full cost. For bookkeeping, a sole trader's own time generally isn't a deductible cost. Rules differ by country and business type, so confirm with an accountant.

What's the difference between COGS and expenses?

COGS is the materials cost of the items you sold. Other expenses (ads, software, booth fees, equipment) are operating expenses. Under the simple cash method, materials purchases are also recorded as expenses when bought.

How do I handle materials I bought in bulk for the whole year?

Under the cash method they're an expense when bought. Under the cost-of-goods method they sit in stock and become COGS as the products that use them sell. Either way, record the purchase and the unit cost.

Do I need to count my stock?

If you use the cost-of-goods method, a count at least at year end keeps your figures honest, and some tax systems expect it. Even under the cash method, a count tells you what's tied up on your shelves.
