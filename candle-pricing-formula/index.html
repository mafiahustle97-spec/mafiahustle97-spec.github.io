The Candle Pricing Formula (Worked Example)

An 8 oz soy candle with $4.45 of materials looks like a healthy product at $12. Count your time, overhead and Etsy's fees and it earns one cent. Here's the candle pricing formula, worked line by line, and what to do when the right price feels too high.

Candles are one of the most popular handmade products, and one of the easiest to underprice. The materials are cheap and visible: wax, fragrance, a wick, a jar. The costs that make or break the product are invisible: the time it takes to pour, cure, clean, label and pack, and the platform fees on every sale.

This guide works through one candle completely, then shows what changes for a smaller tin and for batch production. Every figure is an example, so swap in your own.

The formula in one line

Price = (full cost + fixed fees) ÷ (1 − fee % − target margin)

Full cost = materials + other direct costs + labour + overhead. Round up to .99.

If you want the reasoning behind each layer, it's in how to price handmade products. Here we'll apply it.

Step 1: cost the materials per gram and per ml

Two candle-specific notes:

  • Weigh your wax, don't trust the jar size. An "8 oz" jar describes the jar's volume. The wax that fills it weighs less. Weigh one poured candle and use that.
  • Work out fragrance from your actual load. Fragrance is usually measured as a percentage of wax weight, and suppliers publish a maximum. Whatever your load is, convert it to grams or ml per candle and cost that. It's often the most expensive ingredient per candle after the jar.

Step 2: add your time and overhead

A single candle doesn't take long to pour. But the time that belongs to it also includes melting, wick prep, cleaning jars, curing checks, trimming wicks, labelling, photographing your range and packing the order. Spread across a batch, call it 15 minutes per candle.

LayerCalculationAmount
Materialsfrom the table above$4.45
Labour15 min ÷ 60 × $20/hr$5.00
Overhead($4.45 + $5.00) × 10%$0.95
Full cost per candle$10.40

Materials are $4.45. The candle costs $10.40. That gap is where underpricing lives.

Step 3: add Etsy's fees and your margin

Using our US Etsy defaults as of November 2026 (6.5% transaction + 3% processing = 9.5%; $0.25 per order + $0.20 listing = $0.45 fixed) and a 35% target margin:

($10.40 + $0.45) ÷ (1 − 0.095 − 0.35) = $10.85 ÷ 0.555 = $19.55, rounded up to $19.99.

Example 2: a 4 oz travel tin

Smaller candles are where fixed fees bite. Here's a 4 oz tin, using the same wax and fragrance prices:

LayerCalculationAmount
Wax100 g × $0.0071$0.71
Fragrance9 ml × $0.0507$0.46
Tin with lid, wick, label$0.80 + $0.09 + $0.14$1.03
Materials$2.19
Labour8 min at $20/hr$2.67
Overhead10%$0.49
Full cost$5.34

Suggested Etsy price at 35%: ($5.34 + $0.45) ÷ 0.555 = $10.43, rounded up to $10.99. At $10 the tin makes $3.26 (33%); at $12 it makes $5.07 (42%).

Notice that the $0.45 of fixed fees is 8% of this tin's full cost but only 4% of the 8 oz candle's. That's why low-priced items struggle on platforms with per-order fees, and why sets and bundles help. A trio of tins in one order pays the $0.25 processing fee once.

Example 3: what batching does to the price

Suppose you get your 8 oz process down to 10 minutes per candle by pouring 24 at a time, labelling in one sitting and packing with a set-up station.

  • Labour: 10 min at $20/hr = $3.33
  • Overhead: ($4.45 + $3.33) × 10% = $0.78
  • Full cost: $8.56
  • Suggested price at 35%: ($8.56 + $0.45) ÷ 0.555 = $16.24, rounded up to $16.99

If you keep selling at $19.99, profit rises from $7.24 to $9.08 per candle. Same hourly rate, same materials. Getting faster without cutting your rate is the healthiest way to make a candle line more profitable.

The same candle on other channels

At a craft fair on a card reader (2.6% + $0.15), the suggested price for the $10.40 candle drops to $16.99, and selling at $19.99 there would make $8.92. Don't rush to cut your market price, though. Fairs have booth fees, travel and a full day of your time that this per-item formula doesn't see. Use profit per hour to judge the event as a whole.

Wholesale is a different calculation again. With the common 2×2 rule, this candle's wholesale price would be $20.80 and its suggested retail $41.60, far above what most shops pay for a candle like this. The wholesale pricing guide covers what to do about that.

When $19.99 feels too high

Plenty of makers run the numbers and think "nobody will pay that". Before you drop the price:

  1. Check the inputs. Is 15 minutes realistic, or did you include time that belongs to the whole range? Is your fragrance cost per ml right?
  2. Look at batching (above). Minutes are usually the biggest lever.
  3. Buy bigger. Wax and fragrance in larger units usually cost less per gram. Re-cost when you switch suppliers.
  4. Change the offer. A larger candle, a gift set or a refill option can carry a better price-to-effort ratio.
  5. Compare like with like. Mass-produced candles aren't your price benchmark. Look at shops with similar materials, finish and presentation.
  6. Keep a few products as entry points, but know their real margin, and make sure the rest of the range pays the bills.

Candle pricing checklist

  • Wax weighed per candle, costed per gram
  • Fragrance costed per ml or gram at your actual load
  • Jar, lid, wick, label, warning label and packaging included
  • Labour includes prep, cleaning, labelling and packing, not just pouring
  • Overhead included (10% to start)
  • Channel fees for where it will actually sell
  • Price rounded up, and re-checked when wax or jar prices change
  • No candle sold below full cost, except as a deliberate, counted promotion

Frequently asked questions

What's a typical markup for candles?

We'd rather not quote a market average, because it hides your costs. Work out your full cost, choose a target margin, and let the formula give the price. The worked example's $19.99 is about 4.5 times materials, which shows how misleading "materials × 2 or 3" can be.

Should I include curing time in labour?

No. Curing is waiting, not working. Include the minutes you actually spend: melting, pouring, checking, trimming, labelling and packing.

Do I include failed candles?

Yes, as waste. If one candle in twenty is a second, spread its materials across the nineteen you sell. The cost of goods guide shows how.
